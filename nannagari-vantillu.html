Nannagari Vantillu

Designing a mobile-friendly way to explore an Andhra menu.

A restaurant prototype built around a practical question: how can guests find a dish, compare their options and keep a selection without losing the character of the restaurant?

The starting point

Nannagari Vantillu is an Andhra restaurant project. The supplied menu contains 37 entries across eight categories, including starters, sangati combinations, meals, biryanis and extras. Some items have availability restrictions; daily specials need a confirmed price.

The design challenge was to organise that information for guests with different intentions: someone who knows the dish they want, someone browsing a category, and someone narrowing choices by diet.

My working hypothesis is that clear search, separate dietary filters and familiar categories can make this menu easier to explore. This is informed by the supplied menu and my hospitality background; interviews and usability testing with guests are still to come.

What I wanted the experience to support

  • Find dishes by name, category or dietary preference.
  • Compare prices and availability without opening every item.
  • Save favourites or build a selection without creating an account.
  • Reach useful visit and contact information when needed.

My contribution and use of AI

I set the project direction, reviewed the interface and refined the experience through iterative feedback. AI-assisted development helped turn those decisions into working screens and interactions.

My contribution centred on the information presented to guests, navigation, mobile layout, visual direction and interface wording. AI supported implementation and illustrative food imagery. The prototype uses React Native / Expo and TypeScript, with browser previews of the shared screens.

The decisions behind the interface

Keep dietary choices separate from dish categories

“Veg” and “Biryani” answer different questions. The menu therefore has one set of dietary controls and another for dish categories, alongside text search. Guests can combine them to narrow the same menu.

Conflicting choices lead to an explicit empty state with a way to browse all dishes again. Items without a supplied dietary label remain in “All diets”; the interface does not guess. The meals entry can appear under both veg and non-veg because the menu lists both variants.

Save favourites without sign-in

The current version saves favourites on the device. This keeps account setup out of the browsing flow and lets a guest return to a dish on the same browser or device.

The trade-off: favourites do not sync across devices, and clearing local storage removes them. This keeps the prototype simple while limiting continuity between devices.

Adapting the experience for phones

The earlier mobile exploration devoted most of the first screen to the brand introduction and platter. The refined home brings search, meal information and category shortcuts into view earlier, with compact food rows and persistent Home, Menu and Visit navigation.

The current visual direction uses forest green, ivory and brass. The aim is to retain the restaurant’s identity while giving food discovery more space. This is an interface iteration; its effect on usability has not yet been measured with guests.

Make selection clear before adding checkout

Guests can add dishes, change quantities and keep a selection on the device. The basket calculates a subtotal for priced items. Unpriced daily specials are excluded and flagged for confirmation.

The mobile summary explicitly says “Not sent to the restaurant”. The prototype does not submit orders, process payments or notify staff. Keeping that boundary visible is part of the interface design.

A first review of the experience

This is an AI-assisted, scenario-based review of the prototype screenshots and documented behaviour. It is not feedback from restaurant guests or a live usability test. The points below are hypotheses to check with people.

Finding lunch has a clear starting point

The current mobile screen puts search, a meal price and category shortcuts near the top. These give someone looking for lunch several ways into the menu. I still need to check whether people understand the meal contents and the “Veg & non-veg” label before adding it.

Next step: test whether guests can identify the available meal variants and choose the one they want. If the distinction is unclear, make the variant choice explicit before adding a meal.

The basket wording could promise too much

The dish button says “Add to order”, but the prototype only saves a selection. Even with “Not sent to the restaurant” in the mobile summary, that first label could create an expectation that ordering is available.

Proposed change: try “Add to selection” and keep the unsent status visible in the basket. Ask people to explain what happens next, in their own words. This wording change has not yet been implemented in the restaurant prototype.

Saving a dish needs a clear return path

The heart icon and count offer a compact way to reach favourites. The question is whether a first-time visitor recognises that route and understands that saved dishes stay on the same device.

Proposed change: test a short “Saved on this device” confirmation after the first save. Check whether someone can leave the menu and find that dish again without prompting.

These review notes help prioritise the next round of testing. They do not establish that guests encountered these issues, or that the proposed changes improve the experience.

What is working, and what remains to validate

The result is a working discovery and selection prototype. The automated integration checks passed during preparation of this case study. They cover menu filters, restaurant-local opening hours, delivery URL handling, motion logic, basket restoration, quantities and mixed-price subtotals.

The next usability tasks

I would ask restaurant guests to complete three tasks, then observe their choices, wrong turns and explanations:

  1. Find a vegetarian biryani and check its price.
  2. Save a dish, leave the menu and find the saved item again.
  3. Build a selection containing a daily special, then explain what the subtotal includes and whether the restaurant has received an order.

The findings would guide the next iteration of filter wording, favourites and selection feedback. Before launch, menu accuracy, allergens, final food photography, asset performance and real-device behaviour also need review.

What this project is teaching me

My hospitality background gives me a practical starting point for the information a guest might need. Building the prototype turns that into concrete interface decisions: what should be visible, what can wait and where the wording needs to be explicit.

The mobile iteration also shows why visual character and practical tasks need to be considered together. My next priority is feedback from guests on discovery and selection.
